    How the monk was taken apart

    The classic voice is a source–filter sound in disguise. Each grain is three decaying sine waves, one per formant, restarted every pitch period. That is the impulse response of three resonators struck once per glottal pulse. A pulse train through three resonators is a voice with an idealised throat.

    So the physical engine asks what body has those resonances. A vocal tract is a tube. Its shape (tongue, jaw, lips) sets where it rings. Its length scales every ring together, and the pharynx and mouth scale separately. We fit one body and five mouth shapes so the tube rings at the classic monk's fifteen formants:

    Everyone else is that same gesture performed by another body, not a pitch-shifted copy. A soprano has a shorter tube, with most of the difference in the pharynx. Her folds close softly (head register) and she opens toward "ah" as she climbs, so her first resonance follows her pitch. A countertenor keeps a man's tube but sings with the folds barely touching (falsetto). Every singer has its own glottal jitter, vibrato, pitch wander and breath.

    • Gyuto chant. A fundamental near 65–70 Hz. Each monk tunes F1 onto the 5th harmonic and F2 onto the 10th by listening to his own resonances, so one voice sounds a chord.
    • Half tone (dzo-ke, kargyraa). The ventricular ("false") folds vibrate once for every two glottal pulses and partly block every second one. Flow alternates, and the sub-octave appears.
    • Khöömei. Pressed phonation keeps the glottis closed longer, so resonances ring sharper. The tongue slides one resonance onto a chosen harmonic.
    • Sygyt. The tongue tip seals behind the teeth. The cavity in front clusters F2 and F3 into one narrow peak, the whistle.

    Vowel orbit. Each singer can circle through vowel space: one axis runs ooh–ow–ah–ayh–eeh, the other rounds the lips a quarter-cycle later, so the path is an ellipse. Every singer keeps its own period. They can move together, in small-integer ratios (1 : 3⁄2 : 2 : 5⁄4…), or freely with a chosen spread. Because the motion happens inside each tube, the resonances move physically; no filter is being swept.

    The song (Rol-mo at 140). Monastic ritual music is not harmony led by a conductor. It is independent cycles layered together, and each has a cousin in a bass drop. Dungchen are played in pairs that overlap, so the drone never breaks: here they become the sub-bass and the brass stabs. Rolmo cymbal strokes accelerate into rolls and slow down like a bouncing ball: the build's snare roll is the same gesture forced onto a grid. Two gyaling play one melody with their own ornaments, which becomes the doubled lead. The free ritual pulse settles at 70 BPM, exactly the half-time feel of a 140 BPM drop. The drop's “wobble” is a basso's vocal tract running the vowel orbit at the tempo, growling with the ventricular half tone, while a sygyt singer whistles the hook on harmonics 8–12. The melodies and rhythms are original; the chanted syllables are the traditional om mani padme hum. The instruments are signal models of how each one sounds (lip buzz, double reed, cymbal and membrane modes), not waveguide physics like the voices.

    The fugue (Fugue in C minor for throats). Five minutes built from one original two-bar subject. It is answered at the fifth against a countersubject written for it, and slowed fourfold into a cantus firmus for the monks. It is walked around the circle of fifths in episodes, overlapped with itself one bar apart at the octave over dominant and tonic pedals (stretto), turned upside down, harmonized as four-part chorales by a search over chords and voice leading, and set in canon between the chant and a throat bass. The overtone singer carries two lines at once: for every note it picks a drone and a harmonic of that drone, preferring drones in the current chord. The drops borrow general bass-music technique (call and response, switching timbre within a bar, pitch dives, a short transient near 200 Hz at the head of each call, syncopated gaps) and play it on three throats instead of three synth patches. It ends with a Picardy third, and that major third is the overtone singer's 10th harmonic, the same overtone the Gyuto monks tune to. An audit counts the counterpoint: about 8% of voice pairs are dissonant on strong beats over the whole piece, mostly in the pedal-point builds. A few dozen parallel fifths and octaves remain across five minutes and seven voices.

    The passacaglia (Passacaglia on a Throat Ground). Written after measuring a 30-second clip of bass-music sound design. The measurements were technique, not notes. The sub and the mid-bass are independent layers: their envelopes are uncorrelated. The sub keeps its own phrasing, with a gap of about a beat and a half every two bars. A kick falls about 16 semitones onto the sub's pitch every half bar. The mid-bass talks in held, vowel-like plateaus joined by fast V-shaped resonance sweeps. Parallel descending sweeps fill the gaps, and a high tone is held above with a slow vibrato. Here a throat does each of those things. The parallel sweep is the throat itself getting longer: the larynx lowers and the lips push out, so every formant slides down together. A real tract can only manage about three semitones that way, so it is combined with a vowel sweep. The form is a passacaglia: the descending A–G–F–E ground repeats under sixteen variations, among them a canon in free imitation, the ground rising into the upper voices, the theme sinking into the chant, a chorale, a stretto and three drops. Audit: 6.6% of voice pairs dissonant on strong beats.

    The HDR voice. The physical voice renders the midtones well: harmonics through formants, within about 40 dB of the loudest partial. The HDR tab adds what lives in the shadows and the highlights, and lets the source hear the tube. The tube is linear for a given shape, so it runs three times on the same shape: voiced flow, turbulence, and fold collisions. Their sum is the voice, and each can be soloed without approximation. Five experiments. (1) The glottis prescribes only its opening; the flow follows the pressure across it, with the inertia of the air in the glottis lagging it. (2) Turbulence is born at the narrowest point of the mouth, grows with the jet's speed past a threshold, and is coloured by jet speed over gap width. (3) A contact transient when the folds close, scaled by closing speed squared: pressed voice clicks, falsetto (which never closes) makes none. (4) Transverse resonances of the widest section (1.84, 3.05 and 3.83 × c/πd) driven by the tube's own pressure. (5) One physiological state (breath pressure, laryngeal tremor, heartbeat) moves pitch, loudness and fold pressure together. Measured against the physical voice on the same notes (chest, baritone): harmonics-to-noise ratio 14.8 dB instead of 4.0 dB, energy above 5 kHz −17 dB instead of −5.5 dB, and frame-level pitch–loudness correlation 0.51 instead of −0.01. The comparison is loudness-matched. Measuring it showed that the physical voice's breath noise was shaped as a high-pass, which made it too hissy. The physical voice now uses the HDR shaping too: its chest-voice HNR went from 4 to 21.7 dB.

    Three more layers. Breathing below: a 20 cm subglottal airway (trachea, then the bronchi widening) resonating at 600 / 1410 / 2200 Hz for an adult man, as reported for real airways, and higher for smaller bodies. Coupled only through the flow, it changes the voice by at most 0.4 dB: real subglottal effects would need the two tubes joined acoustically through the open glottis. Yielding walls: when the lips or tongue seal, the flow fills the mouth slowly, the walls hum at about −22 dB under the vowel (the voice bar), and the trapped pressure bursts at the release; without the walls the closure is silent and there is no burst. Articulator inertia: each articulator is a damped mass with its own speed. In "ah→ee" F2 now lags F1 by 16 ms (3 ms without), and the formant path bends more. The turbulence layer also had to learn that two narrowings in series share one flow: the jet speed saturates as a gap closes instead of running away.

    The room. Under the stage, choose a room. Each seat of singers gets its own early reflections from an image-source model of a box with real surface materials, heard from a near-coincident pair of cardioid microphones. The late tail decays per band at the rate predicted for that room's size and surfaces (Eyring), and its level comes from the room constant. Measured mid-band decay matches the prediction: 8.6 s against 8.7 s in the stone hall, 2.2 against 2.1 in the temple. The floor is modelled three ways. Its first reflection is filtered by the floor's reflection at the actual angle. Monks sit close to it, so it arrives a few milliseconds after the direct sound and cuts comb notches exactly where the path difference predicts: 630 / 1900 / 3160 Hz seated against a predicted 646 / 1938 / 3230, moving to 370 Hz when they stand. Stone notches run 11–14 dB deep; carpet and earth absorb the reflection and stay under 6 dB. Hard floors facing hard ceilings flutter. A wooden dais is a plate with its own bending modes (38, 54, 72 Hz…) that the seated singers drive through their bodies: about +4 dB of body below 200 Hz. Rooms are loudness-matched, and in the songs the electronic drop stays dry while the monks and instruments sit in the room. Not modelled: the spherical-wave ground wave that makes porous ground dip at grazing angles outdoors.

    Throat singing. Select a monk and open Throat under its controls; the new ensembles show each mechanism. Ventricular folds can move once for every one, two or three glottal cycles. Measured against the fundamental, one-in-two puts a sub-octave at −12 dB, one-in-three a sub-twelfth (f0/3) at −18 dB with no sub-octave, and moving in step adds no sub-harmonic, only a harder pulse. Press lengthens the closed phase and seals the folds faster, so resonances ring sharper. The older overtone tuners listen to one resonance and push one articulator. Three new gestures work differently: the singer carries a map of its own mouth, the exact response of the whole tube (lips and nostrils together) over a plane of two movements, and moves to the shape where the wanted harmonic stands highest above its neighbours. That is what lets a singer tune through the nose with the lips sealed: there the tongue reshapes a hidden side cavity, and no single resonance moves smoothly. Over a 110 Hz drone, measured in the rendered sound (more than 6 dB above both neighbours): lips 7–13, nose 5–11, tongue root 4–11. The kargyraa vowel walk tunes onto harmonics of the sub-octave. Even ones (also harmonics of the sung note) ring out by 9–14 dB, odd ones much less, because the source puts little energy there. The selected monk's map is drawn in the inspector. Overtone melodies step the target harmonic; an offset sets monks in canon. Ornaments: a harmonic trill in which the tongue rocks between neighbours (the rolling of borbangnadyr), lip flutter, a tongue-tip trill, and a galloping triple lip pulse (the stirrup rhythm of ezengileer). Breathing stops each phrase for an audible in-breath (−15 dB under the voice), then the overtone climbs back into place. Breath games alternate out-breath and in-breath, voiced and breathed steps. For the in-breath the flow reverses and the glottal pulse is mirrored in time; the mirroring is a modelling assumption, and it changes the spectrum only slightly (−4 dB above 2 kHz). The styles and ensembles are named after Tuvan, Mongolian, Sardinian, Xhosa and Inuit practice as mechanical sketches. They are not transcriptions of those traditions, and every melody and motif is original. Building them exposed clicks: the tube switched its lip opening instantly while carrying a steady airflow. It now moves the opening smoothly, which removed half to two thirds of the sudden jumps in the three songs without changing their balance (bands within 0.2 dB).

    A more complete throat. Three mechanisms are now modelled from the anatomy rather than drawn as shapes, and each is checked against measurements of real singers.

    Focus. MRI of Tuvan singers (Bergevin and colleagues, 2020) shows two narrowings of the tongue in sygyt. One, at the ridge behind the teeth (about 13 cm from the glottis), merges the second and third resonances into one sharp peak; the other, further back, moves that peak onto the wanted harmonic. Their recordings put the neighbouring harmonics 15–35 dB under the sung one, and the next ones out 35–65 dB under. Our older gestures reached 9–19 dB (sygyt) and 6–17 dB (khöömei). The new focus gesture was learned offline: for 25 frequencies from 0.8 to 2.6 kHz, it is the shape (out of 142 155 two-narrowing shapes) that lifts a harmonic furthest above its neighbours, chosen so neighbouring shapes stay close. The front narrowing landed at 11–13 cm without being told where. Its peaks are only about 20 Hz wide, which exposed a flaw in the mouth map: its frequency grid was coarser than the peaks, and a harmonic between two learned shapes lost 10 dB. The singer now feels its way to the exact spot. It starts from the map's choice and tries small moves of both narrowings, scoring each with the tube's exact response, about twenty shapes per tuning step. Sung, every harmonic 6–18 over 110–147 Hz stands 15–27 dB above its neighbours and 14–33 dB above the next ones out: inside the measured range for the neighbours, short of it two away. The same refinement lifted the lip, nose and tongue-root gestures as well. Air rushing through the near-closed gap now also hisses, about 21 dB under the whistle above 3 kHz.

    Self-oscillating folds (choose it per monk under Throat → Larynx). The vocal folds are two masses on springs and the false folds a third. Air from the lungs flows through both openings in series. The pressure between the two pairs pushes on the folds, the folds move, the openings and the flow change. No pulse shape is drawn: pitch, closure and the false folds' behaviour come out of that loop. The singer holds pitch by tension, listening to its folds' swing (90–300 Hz held to 0.1 Hz). High-speed films of kargyraa and of the Sardinian bassu show the false folds touching on every second glottal cycle and the glottal closures alternating fast and slow (Lindestad and colleagues 2001; Henrich, Lortat-Jacob, Castellengo, Bailly and Pelorson 2006). The model was not told to do either. With the false folds drawn in and their tension below half the pitch, they touch on 49–51% of cycles over 87–123 Hz drones, the pitch stays within 6 cents, the closures alternate, and the sub-harmonic series carries as much energy as the harmonics (the drawn pulse: 7 dB less). The air through their gap stiffens them, so they lock while set slacker than half the pitch. The tension is chosen by pitch from a measured map of the regimes. Limits: the locking holds from about 70 to 150 Hz (above 120 Hz it needs more lung pressure); at 200 Hz, the bassu's pitch, the trapped air stops the vocal folds; a one-in-three lock never emerged, so the sub-twelfth stays on the drawn pulse. The breath games' in-breath also stays on the drawn pulse.

    Steppe Cycle. A suite of throat-singing styles over one D3 drone, whose harmonics 6, 8, 9, 10, 12 and 16 make a major pentatonic. Each section chooses those harmonics a different way: khöömei (the tongue follows one resonance), ezengileer (a sygyt whistle with the lips galloping on it), dumchuktaar (through the nose, from the singer's map of its mouth), borbangnadyr (the tongue rocking between neighbours) and a three-gesture canon, sygyt (the two-narrowing focus), and a kargyraa whose false folds lock onto every second cycle on their own (measured in the rendered song: 0.50 contacts per cycle, dipping only at his breaths). The jaw harp (khomus) is a physical voice too: a steel-reed "glottis" (a very narrow, steady pulse at a fixed pitch) in front of a mouth that picks its harmonics with the lips. The lute (doshpuluur, plucked strings as loops that lose their brightness first) and the frame drum (tüngür) are signal models. Balancing it showed how much the whistle depends on its surroundings: alone it stood 21 dB above its neighbouring harmonics, in the first mix 5 dB, because the lute's plucks on D and A put energy on the same harmonics; with the lute softer and darker it stands 12 dB above them.

    Tenore and the Breath Game. Two traditions that both make music from the throat's mechanics, in the manner of (not transcribed from) Sardinian cantu a tenore and Inuit katajjaq, set against each other and finally against a drop. The bassu sings on self-oscillating folds whose false folds lock onto every second cycle by themselves (0.50 contacts per cycle through the piece), so his F2 is heard as F1; the contra's two pairs of folds move in step; the boghe leads. Two women trade breath motifs one step apart on the out-breath and in-breath, voiced and breathed, accelerating from 3.4 to 7.7 steps a second until one breaks off; later their motifs, retuned to each chord, become the drop's percussion while the sub doubles the bassu at the octave his false folds make. Melodies, progressions and motifs are original.

    Om of Ten Throats. A slow ritual piece where the new mechanisms carry the form. The Gyuto monks change chords without changing pitch: each moves its two resonances to other harmonics of its C2 (5 and 10, then 4 and 8, 4 and 10, 6 and 10, 6 and 9). Doubled voices start each phrase on the m of Om; when the lips open the false folds lock (0.50 contacts per cycle within about half a second) and on the closing m they let go (0.00). A nasal singer sings through the hum of the Om, and three focus whistles on one drone form a chorale of harmonics (8-10-12 is a major triad inside one note), each standing about 20 dB over its neighbours. Composing it found real limits: some Gyuto pairs (5/12, 7/14) are out of the tube's reach, and above about 1.5 kHz the focus whistle is much quieter, so the chorale sits on G2.

    Two fixes to the tube. Writing these pieces found two old faults. After a hum the velum never quite closed, and the one-section velum port, open at both ends, quietly integrated the throat's pressure, a thousandfold, then fed it back through its tiny area: every "m" left clicks behind it (up to 0.43 at a voice peak of 0.24). The port now loses energy in proportion to how narrow it is (as a narrow duct's boundary layer does), and the junction where the nose joins now moves smoothly with the shape like every other junction. And moving a near-closed constriction quickly (the focus whistle's leaps) pumped energy into the tube (peaks of 2.7 against a steady 0.08), because a narrow section's large pressures carry little power until it widens. The tube now stores power-normalised waves, so every junction is a rotation and conserves energy however the shape moves; for a fixed shape it is the same tube (closed forms still exact to 10−13). The same leaps now peak at 0.45 with no jumps beyond a steady whistle's; the older songs lost a third to a half of their sudden jumps with their balance unchanged.
