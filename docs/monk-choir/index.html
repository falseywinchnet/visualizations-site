    How the monk was taken apart

    The classic voice is a source–filter sound in disguise. Each grain is three decaying sine waves, one per formant, restarted every pitch period. That is the impulse response of three resonators struck once per glottal pulse. A pulse train through three resonators is a voice with an idealised throat.

    So the physical engine asks what body has those resonances. A vocal tract is a tube. Its shape (tongue, jaw, lips) sets where it rings. Its length scales every ring together, and the pharynx and mouth scale separately. We fit one body and five mouth shapes so the tube rings at the classic monk's fifteen formants:

    Everyone else is that same gesture performed by another body, not a pitch-shifted copy. A soprano has a shorter tube, with most of the difference in the pharynx. Her folds close softly (head register) and she opens toward "ah" as she climbs, so her first resonance follows her pitch. A countertenor keeps a man's tube but sings with the folds barely touching (falsetto). Every singer has its own glottal jitter, vibrato, pitch wander and breath.

    • Gyuto chant. A fundamental near 65–70 Hz. Each monk tunes F1 onto the 5th harmonic and F2 onto the 10th by listening to his own resonances, so one voice sounds a chord.
    • Half tone (dzo-ke, kargyraa). The ventricular ("false") folds vibrate once for every two glottal pulses and partly block every second one. Flow alternates, and the sub-octave appears.
    • Khöömei. Pressed phonation keeps the glottis closed longer, so resonances ring sharper. The tongue slides one resonance onto a chosen harmonic.
    • Sygyt. The tongue tip seals behind the teeth. The cavity in front clusters F2 and F3 into one narrow peak, the whistle.

    Vowel orbit. Each singer can circle through vowel space: one axis runs ooh–ow–ah–ayh–eeh, the other rounds the lips a quarter-cycle later, so the path is an ellipse. Every singer keeps its own period. They can move together, in small-integer ratios (1 : 3⁄2 : 2 : 5⁄4…), or freely with a chosen spread. Because the motion happens inside each tube, the resonances move physically; no filter is being swept.

    The song (Rol-mo at 140). Monastic ritual music is not harmony led by a conductor. It is independent cycles layered together, and each has a cousin in a bass drop. Dungchen are played in pairs that overlap, so the drone never breaks: here they become the sub-bass and the brass stabs. Rolmo cymbal strokes accelerate into rolls and slow down like a bouncing ball: the build's snare roll is the same gesture forced onto a grid. Two gyaling play one melody with their own ornaments, which becomes the doubled lead. The free ritual pulse settles at 70 BPM, exactly the half-time feel of a 140 BPM drop. The drop's “wobble” is a basso's vocal tract running the vowel orbit at the tempo, growling with the ventricular half tone, while a sygyt singer whistles the hook on harmonics 8–12. The melodies and rhythms are original; the chanted syllables are the traditional om mani padme hum. The instruments are signal models of how each one sounds (lip buzz, double reed, cymbal and membrane modes), not waveguide physics like the voices.

    The fugue (Fugue in C minor for throats). Five minutes built from one original two-bar subject. It is answered at the fifth against a countersubject written for it, and slowed fourfold into a cantus firmus for the monks. It is walked around the circle of fifths in episodes, overlapped with itself one bar apart at the octave over dominant and tonic pedals (stretto), turned upside down, harmonized as four-part chorales by a search over chords and voice leading, and set in canon between the chant and a throat bass. The overtone singer carries two lines at once: for every note it picks a drone and a harmonic of that drone, preferring drones in the current chord. The drops borrow general bass-music technique (call and response, switching timbre within a bar, pitch dives, a short transient near 200 Hz at the head of each call, syncopated gaps) and play it on three throats instead of three synth patches. It ends with a Picardy third, and that major third is the overtone singer's 10th harmonic, the same overtone the Gyuto monks tune to. An audit counts the counterpoint: about 8% of voice pairs are dissonant on strong beats over the whole piece, mostly in the pedal-point builds. A few dozen parallel fifths and octaves remain across five minutes and seven voices.

    The passacaglia (Passacaglia on a Throat Ground). Written after measuring a 30-second clip of bass-music sound design. The measurements were technique, not notes. The sub and the mid-bass are independent layers: their envelopes are uncorrelated. The sub keeps its own phrasing, with a gap of about a beat and a half every two bars. A kick falls about 16 semitones onto the sub's pitch every half bar. The mid-bass talks in held, vowel-like plateaus joined by fast V-shaped resonance sweeps. Parallel descending sweeps fill the gaps, and a high tone is held above with a slow vibrato. Here a throat does each of those things. The parallel sweep is the throat itself getting longer: the larynx lowers and the lips push out, so every formant slides down together. A real tract can only manage about three semitones that way, so it is combined with a vowel sweep. The form is a passacaglia: the descending A–G–F–E ground repeats under sixteen variations, among them a canon in free imitation, the ground rising into the upper voices, the theme sinking into the chant, a chorale, a stretto and three drops. Audit: 6.6% of voice pairs dissonant on strong beats.

    Numbers checked by the test suite in this page's source: the closed-form tube response matches the time-domain waveguide to 10−13; scaling a body by 0.8 raises every resonance by exactly 1.25; Gyuto locking settles at 327/654 Hz for a 65.4 Hz fundamental; khöömei holds harmonics 8–12 of 110 Hz at 8–10 dB above their neighbours, and sygyt holds 10–12 at 17 dB.
